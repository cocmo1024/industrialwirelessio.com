# Alarm Latching, Last-Known State, and Local Buffering for Unattended Sites

When a remote site loses communications, two failures often happen at once. The link is gone, and the operating story disappears with it. Operators no longer know which alarm came first, whether the last reported state was stable, or whether the site continued doing the expected thing after the signal vanished. For unattended telemetry, preserving that local story is a major part of reliability.

## What matters first

Sites usually need stronger local continuity when:

- dispatch is expensive or slow;
- alarms drive time-sensitive response;
- intermittent communications are normal rather than rare;
- the difference between "link lost" and "site abnormal" matters operationally.

In those environments, local buffering, alarm latching, and last-known-state logic often create more real resilience than another layer of dashboard polish.

## What this page is for

Use this page when the team needs:

- better continuity of information during link loss;
- a practical way to preserve alarm and state context locally;
- guidance on what should be latched versus what can be reconstructed later;
- a reliability model that helps remote operators act with confidence.

## What should be preserved locally

| Local continuity element | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Last known operating state | Gives operators a baseline before the site went dark |
| Latched critical alarms | Prevents important events from disappearing during intermittent links |
| Time-ordered buffer of recent transitions | Helps reconstruct what happened before and during communications loss |
| Key health indicators | Shows whether the site likely remained stable or degraded |

That set is usually enough to preserve the site's operational story.

## When alarm latching matters most

Alarm latching becomes important when:

- the site may briefly regain and lose communications repeatedly;
- the alarm condition could clear before operators see it remotely;
- dispatch decisions depend on whether the event truly happened;
- remote staff need a durable indication that a critical state occurred.

Without latching, intermittent links can make serious sites look calmer than they really were.

## How much buffering is enough

The goal is not infinite retention. The goal is enough local history to:

- reconstruct the most recent meaningful transitions;
- understand the order of alarm, recovery, and link-loss events;
- preserve action-driving evidence until the remote system catches up.

More buffering is only useful if the operations team can actually use it.

## Common failure modes

These sites usually become frustrating when:

- link loss wipes away the site's recent event history;
- alarms are not latched and disappear during intermittent coverage;
- the remote team cannot tell whether the site failed or only the link failed;
- buffering exists but the operational workflow never references it.

The result is repeated uncertainty and avoidable dispatches.

## A latching policy by signal class

Write the policy per signal class, not per site, and the arguments mostly settle themselves. Safety and damage signals — high-high level, gas detection, fire, intrusion at critical sites — latch, always: their transient occurrence is the fact that matters, and nobody should need to be watching at the right second. Process excursions with operational consequences — pump fault, low pressure sustained, power fail — latch until acknowledged, because the response workflow needs a queue that does not evaporate. Diagnostic and quality signals — low battery warning, signal-strength degradation, enclosure temperature — self-clear but count: a self-clearing signal that fires forty times a month is a maintenance fact, and the count should surface in reporting even though no single occurrence deserved an alarm. Momentary states with no consequence — a door opened during a logged visit, a float bouncing in turbulence — neither latch nor alarm; they belong in the event buffer, available for reconstruction, invisible to the alarm queue.

The anti-pattern to design against is the uniform policy: everything latching drowns operators in acknowledgements until acknowledgement becomes a reflex; everything self-clearing erases exactly the fleeting events that unattended sites exist to capture. The class table, reviewed after the first season against what actually fired, is the deliverable.

## Acknowledgement is a workflow, not a button

Latching only works if acknowledgement means something. Define who may acknowledge, what acknowledgement asserts (a human has seen this, owns it, and the response clock is running), and what happens to a latched alarm nobody acknowledges within its escalation window. Sites also need the honest reverse path: an alarm acknowledged in error should be re-armable without a site visit, and the audit trail should keep both events. When acknowledgement is a reflex — hundreds of latches cleared in a batch each morning — the policy table, not the operator, is the defect: too many signal classes are latching, and the seasonal review should demote them.

## Last-known state: the third option

Between latching and self-clearing sits the pattern unattended sites need most and configure least: last-known state with age. The host displays the final value the site reported, marked with its age, and treats the combination — value plus age — as the operational fact. A tank level last known at 62 percent, aged three hours, during a known carrier outage, is a usable input to a delivery decision; the same value presented without its age is a trap. Buffering completes the pattern: when the link returns, the buffered sequence replaces the flat line, and the reconstruction shows what the level actually did. Latching decides what demands attention; last-known state decides what can still be trusted; buffering decides what can be proven afterwards. A serious site designs all three, per signal class, in the same table.

## Implementation checklist

Before finalizing the reliability model, confirm that:

- the site preserves the minimum state and alarm story needed for remote action;
- critical alarms remain visible after intermittent outages;
- the remote team can interpret buffered events without guessing;
- buffering and latching are tied to real operating decisions, not only theoretical completeness.
