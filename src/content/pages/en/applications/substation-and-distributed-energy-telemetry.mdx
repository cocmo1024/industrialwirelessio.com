# Substation and Distributed Energy Telemetry

Substations and distributed energy sites need remote visibility that stays useful under real field constraints. The challenge is not only communications. It is the combination of event criticality, site survivability, and support burden across assets that may be sparse, weather-exposed, and expensive to visit.

## What matters first

The best designs start by separating:

1. the small set of critical remote events that drive action;
2. the broader operating data that supports planning and diagnosis;
3. the physical and communications stack needed to keep the site visible under harsh field conditions.

That usually produces a simpler, more durable telemetry model than trying to move every possible point the same way.

## Why this application is different

Distributed energy and substation environments often combine:

- outage-sensitive assets;
- uneven field access;
- high consequences for missed alarms;
- pressure to reduce service visits while increasing visibility.

That makes reliability, alarm discipline, and supportability central design choices, not afterthoughts.

## What data should come first

The first remote telemetry layer should usually answer:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Is the asset available and in the expected operating state? | Supports basic operational confidence |
| Did an event occur that needs human action? | Drives dispatch or escalation decisions |
| Is communications loss itself meaningful right now? | Prevents blind spots during critical periods |
| Can the team assess site health without visiting? | Reduces avoidable truck rolls |

That is usually enough to define the first durable architecture.

## The architecture that usually holds up

For many remote energy assets, the durable pattern is:

- local control stays local;
- telemetry is scoped around action-driving events and a few health indicators;
- buffering and store-and-forward behavior are explicit;
- the power, enclosure, and surge design are treated as part of the telemetry stack;
- the network path is chosen around confidence, not theoretical performance.

This is why good field telemetry architecture often looks conservative. Conservative designs survive.

## Common failure modes

These projects usually disappoint when:

- the design assumes communications are always available;
- alarm priority is weak;
- too much low-value data obscures critical events;
- physical deployment details are treated as procurement details rather than system design;
- support ownership across operations and field teams is vague.

The result is a site that is connected but not operationally dependable.

## When more architecture is justified

Broader telemetry and data reuse become more defensible when:

- the action-driving event model is already trusted;
- communications behavior under field stress is understood;
- power and survivability planning are proven;
- the support team can manage the stack without repeated specialist intervention.

Until then, simpler architectures usually create more real value.

## The wireless connection pattern for substations and distributed energy

Substation telemetry divides into two traffic classes with different wireless answers. Operational telemetry — breaker states, transformer temperature and load, battery plant health, intrusion and environmental alarms — is low-rate and alarm-driven, well served by an RTU-class device on cellular or private radio with strict exception rules. Distributed-energy sites (solar, storage, small generation) add a second class: production and power-quality data that owners and aggregators want at minute resolution, which pushes the design toward always-on backhaul and a data budget planned for it.

The boundary discipline matters more than the radio choice. Substation networks are protection-adjacent: the telemetry path must be architecturally separated from protection and control systems, with remote access brokered, logged, and disabled by default. Wireless earns its place at the fence line and the auxiliary systems first — monitoring that never touches the protection chain — and only enters deeper with an explicit security architecture behind it.

Event evidence is the third design point. When a breaker operates, the value of the telemetry is the sequence — what changed first, by how much, over what interval. Local timestamping and buffering at the substation device, delivered after the fact if the link was down, is worth more than a faster live feed that loses the order.

## Battery plant and auxiliary systems: the quiet wins

The highest-value substation telemetry is often the least glamorous: the station battery plant. Charger failure and battery degradation are leading causes of protection systems failing to operate on demand, and they announce themselves slowly — float voltage drift, rising impedance, temperature divergence between cells — to any monitoring patient enough to trend them. A telemetry package that watches the battery plant, the cabinet environment, and the fence line will prevent more serious events than one that streams primary plant data nobody trends. Start the wireless scope there, where the data is slow, the alarms are meaningful, and the security boundary is easiest to defend.

## Time synchronisation is evidence quality

Substation records are compared against relay records, neighbouring sites and the control centre's log after every significant event, and those comparisons stand or fall on timestamps. A telemetry device whose clock drifts minutes per month quietly corrupts every sequence it reports. Specify the time source (network time over the backhaul, GPS where justified), monitor clock health as a data point, and treat a site with a sick clock as a site with sick evidence — its data may still alarm correctly, but it will not reconstruct anything.

Specify the time source before the first device is ordered; retrofitting clock discipline across an installed fleet is nobody's idea of a small job.

## Implementation checklist

Before broadening the site stack, confirm that:

- critical events are explicitly defined;
- communications loss behavior is understood;
- the site remains useful during imperfect coverage;
- the physical deployment stack has been reviewed with the same rigor as the network choice;
- support ownership is explicit after go-live.
