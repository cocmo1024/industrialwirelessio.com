## How to choose a wireless I/O module

Nine criteria decide the shortlist, in the order engineers actually have to settle them: I/O types and count, update rate and latency, network form, band and licensing, range against a real site survey, power source, fail-safe behaviour, hazardous-area and ingress requirements, and the host interface. The decision order matters: **count I/O and update rate first, choose the network form second, and compare brands and models last.**

The full criteria set — each criterion with what to decide and why it decides the shortlist — is canonical on the category page: [Wireless remote I/O and RTU modules](/product-families/wireless-remote-io-and-rtu-modules/). The numbers belong to individual records in the table above, where each one carries its manufacturer source.

## Module, RTU or gateway: three different jobs

A wireless I/O module moves field signals where the wires would have run. An RTU adds local control logic, logging and scheduled reporting at a remote site. A telemetry gateway translates and forwards between networks. Many products combine two of the roles; the selection question is which role is missing from your architecture. The full comparison is canonical with the criteria above on the [wireless remote I/O category page](/product-families/wireless-remote-io-and-rtu-modules/).

## What a telemetry gateway is

A telemetry gateway sits between a field network and a host system and does four jobs: **protocol conversion**, **local buffering** with store-and-forward behaviour so readings survive a backhaul outage, **alarm handling** at the edge, and **backhaul management**. A router moves IP packets without understanding the industrial protocols inside them; a gateway is a translator that happens to buffer. The category definition, the capability floor and the gateway records are on the [industrial telemetry gateways category page](/product-families/industrial-telemetry-gateways/).

## How this database is built

Each record is one product, transcribed from one manufacturer primary source — the datasheet where one is published, the official product documentation where it is not — and the record names that source and the date it was checked. A record earns a full specification page when its source set is complete enough to fill the wireless identity and at least one specification table with sourced values; until then it appears as a row here and in the protocol tables, linked to its source. Specifications are added model by model, never scraped from aggregators and never averaged between sources.
