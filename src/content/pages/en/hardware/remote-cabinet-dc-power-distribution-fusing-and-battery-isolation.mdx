# Remote Cabinet DC Power Distribution, Fusing, and Battery Isolation

Remote telemetry cabinets often fail in ordinary ways that never show up in architecture diagrams. A small short, poor fuse placement, unclear battery isolation path, or badly grouped low-voltage loads can turn a manageable cabinet problem into a full site outage. In unattended environments, power distribution is not just wiring. It is part of the telemetry reliability model.

## What matters first

The strongest cabinet power layouts usually do three things well:

- separate critical and non-critical loads clearly;
- make fault isolation possible without collapsing the whole cabinet;
- protect the battery and charging path from accidental misuse or field confusion.

This matters more over time than adding another accessory or marginal feature inside the cabinet.

## What this page is for

Use this page when the team needs:

- a practical cabinet-power design review for remote telemetry;
- better fault containment in low-voltage field cabinets;
- a clearer approach to fused distribution and battery isolation;
- more serviceable cabinet layouts for unattended assets.

## The cabinet-power design questions that matter

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Which loads are truly critical to keep alive? | Determines what should be protected first |
| What should fail locally without taking down the whole site? | Improves field fault containment |
| Can a technician isolate battery, charger, and branch circuits safely? | Reduces service mistakes |
| Is the power layout obvious to the next person opening the cabinet? | Lowers long-term support risk |

Power design that cannot answer those questions usually becomes fragile in the field.

## Where unattended sites get into trouble

Problems usually appear when:

- all cabinet loads are treated as equally critical;
- branch protection is minimal or inconsistent;
- battery isolation is unclear during service;
- accessory growth turns a clean cabinet into improvised wiring;
- the field team inherits a layout that only the installer understands.

These are common remote-site failure patterns, not rare mistakes.

## What a stronger distribution layout looks like

A stronger low-voltage cabinet usually includes:

- explicit separation of essential telemetry, communications, and secondary accessory loads;
- branch-level protection where one fault should not take the cabinet dark;
- clear service isolation for battery and charger paths;
- documentation and labeling that survive turnover and emergency service.

That is what makes remote cabinets maintainable.

## Common failure modes

These cabinets often disappoint when:

- fuse strategy is generic rather than tied to consequence;
- the battery path is difficult to isolate cleanly;
- optional loads accumulate without revisiting the power model;
- wiring convenience overrides serviceability;
- the site has no clear rule for what should stay powered during degraded conditions.

## Working the load budget

The load budget is arithmetic, and it should be done in amp-hours per day, not in catalogue watts. List every load with its duty: the router and RTU run continuously; the transmitter loops draw their standing current; the heater and fan cycle with weather; the radio's transmit peaks average out to a small daily figure at telemetry duty cycles but dominate the peak-current sizing. Sum the continuous loads, apply honest duty factors to the cyclic ones, and the daily amp-hour figure falls out. Battery capacity is then that figure multiplied by the autonomy days the site must survive, corrected for temperature and for the depth of discharge the battery chemistry tolerates — and then checked against the worst month, not the average one.

A worked shape (illustrative, not a specification): a cabinet drawing 0.8 A continuously at 24 V consumes about 19 Ah per day. Three days of autonomy at 50 percent depth of discharge needs roughly 115 Ah of nameplate capacity before temperature correction. The point of the example is the method: any site whose budget cannot be written in this form is not yet designed, whatever its purchase order says.

## Battery isolation and maintenance safety

Isolation is a safety feature before it is a convenience. A clearly identified battery disconnect — reachable without tools in the dark, labelled, and positioned so a technician can make the cabinet safe before working — prevents the classic injuries and the classic mistakes: the shorted spanner, the reversed replacement battery, the "dead" cabinet that was only ever isolated at the charger. Pair the disconnect with per-branch fusing sized to the conductor, not to the load's appetite, and put the fuse schedule inside the door. When a branch fuse blows at an unattended site, the telemetry should say which branch went dark; a cabinet that reports "power fail" for a single fused sensor loop sends crews out with the wrong parts and the wrong theory.

## Charger behaviour matters too

Size the charger for two jobs at once: carrying the standing load and recovering a depleted battery in a time the site can tolerate, without cooking the battery in the process. A charger that barely exceeds the load current leaves a site one long outage away from a battery that never refills before the next event; a charger with aggressive bulk rates shortens battery life in float service. Temperature-compensated charging is not a refinement at outdoor cabinets — a battery floated at a fixed voltage through a hot summer and a cold winter is being overcharged in one season and undercharged in the other.

## Implementation checklist

Before freezing the cabinet design, confirm that:

- critical and non-critical loads are intentionally separated;
- fuse and branch strategy supports realistic field fault isolation;
- battery and charger isolation are explicit;
- cabinet labeling and drawings are usable for future service;
- the layout still looks reasonable after support burden is included.
