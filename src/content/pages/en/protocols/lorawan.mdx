## LoRa and LoRaWAN are two different layers

Most confusion about this technology starts with the name covering two things. **LoRa** is a radio modulation — chirp spread spectrum, developed by Semtech — that trades data rate for sensitivity: by spreading a signal and integrating it over a long symbol time, a LoRa receiver can decode transmissions far below the noise levels that defeat conventional narrowband radios. **LoRaWAN** is the LoRa Alliance's specification for everything above that radio: how devices join a network, how they are addressed and encrypted, the three device classes, the regional channel plans and payload ceilings, and the architecture in which gateways are simple relays forwarding traffic to a central network server that owns the intelligence.

The architecture is a **star-of-stars**. End devices transmit in a single hop to any gateway in range — there is no mesh and no routing through other devices — and gateways relay those frames over ordinary IP backhaul to the network server, which deduplicates copies received by several gateways, manages each device's data rate and transmit power through ADR (adaptive data rate), and passes application data onward. A device is therefore never "connected" to a particular gateway; it is heard by whichever gateways its link budget reaches, which is what makes coverage a matter of gateway placement rather than network planning in the mesh sense.

## The three device classes decide what a device can hear

Every LoRaWAN device implements **Class A**: it transmits when it has something to say, then opens two short receive windows (1 and 2 seconds after the uplink) before going back to sleep. Downlink traffic — commands, acknowledgements, configuration — can only arrive inside those windows, so a Class A device that reports hourly can be commanded at most hourly. Class A is also where the battery life comes from: the receiver, the expensive part energetically, is off almost all the time.

**Class B** adds beacon-synchronized receive slots: gateways broadcast a time beacon and devices open extra scheduled windows, buying bounded downlink latency (the specification's ping-slot structure runs to a maximum latency of 128 seconds) at a modest power cost. **Class C** keeps the receiver open whenever the device is not transmitting — downlink latency effectively disappears, and so does battery operation: the Alliance's own description puts the receiver's draw at up to about 50 mW, which is why Class C is for mains-powered points. In industrial practice, battery telemetry is Class A, actuated points are rare and usually wired or Class C, and any design that assumes prompt downlink to a battery device has already failed on paper.

## The limits that actually govern an industrial design

The payload table above is the page to design against, and it teaches the central fact about LoRaWAN: **the ceiling depends on the data rate, and the data rate depends on where the device sits.** A sensor deep inside a plant will be served at a robust, slow setting — DR0 to DR2 in Europe — where the application payload ceiling is 51 bytes and the bit rate is 250 to 980 bit/s. A device with a clean path to a rooftop gateway may run DR5, with 222 bytes and 5,470 bit/s. Both are "LoRaWAN range"; they are different products. Application design follows: a reading must fit in tens of bytes at the worst position, so LoRaWAN payloads are engineered values — a few process values, a status word, a counter — not documents.

Europe adds a second, harder constraint: **duty cycle**. The EU863-870 band plan operates under ETSI duty-cycled transmission rules; the default channels carry a limit of under 1%, which is at most about 36 seconds of airtime per hour for any transmitter — including the gateway, whose downlink budget is therefore a shared, scarce resource across every device it serves. The United States replaces the duty cycle with a **400 ms dwell-time** cap per uplink, which is what crushes US payload sizes at slow data rates (11 application bytes at DR0) regardless of how much the application would like to send. Neither limit is a vendor choice or a firmware setting; both are the regulatory price of the licence-free band, and any capacity plan that ignores them is fiction.

Acknowledgements deserve the same realism. A confirmed uplink that is not acknowledged inside its two receive windows is retried by the device (the specification's default is a single transmission, configurable via NbTrans), and every retry spends the same scarce airtime. In a healthy design, confirmed traffic is reserved for the few messages whose loss matters — alarm state changes, daily totals — and routine readings go unconfirmed, with the application tolerating gaps because the next reading is coming anyway.

## Range, stated honestly

This hub does not publish a maximum LoRaWAN range for industrial sites, because the specification does not contain one and the environment decides it. What exists on the record: the LoRa Alliance states **up to 15 km in rural areas**. Semtech, describing the LoRa physical layer, claims connections up to 30 miles apart in rural areas. Both statements carry their condition in plain sight — *rural* — meaning elevated antennas, open paths and slow data rates. Inside a plant, the same physics runs the other way: steel cladding reflects and absorbs, vessels shadow whole areas, and reinforced concrete floors separate levels into different radio worlds. Experienced integrators plan in-plant LoRaWAN as gateway cells per building or per area, verify by survey with a gateway in its intended position, and treat any per-site kilometre figure — including optimistic ones in vendor decks — as a test condition, not a design input.

Two levers genuinely move range. **Antenna height and placement** dominate everything else: a gateway antenna above roofline clutter hears devices that an in-cabinet gateway never will. **Spreading factor** is the second: stepping from SF7 to SF12 buys substantial link budget and is exactly what ADR does automatically for distant devices — at the price of airtime, which is why a network full of SF12 devices has a fraction of the capacity of a network at SF7. Range and capacity are the same budget spent two ways.

## Running a private industrial network

Most industrial LoRaWAN is private: the operator owns the gateways and the network server, and treats both as plant infrastructure. That ownership is the technology's real industrial feature — coverage, capacity and data stay inside the fence, and no operator's roadmap can retire your band plan — but it also means the operator inherits the network engineering that a public operator would otherwise hide.

**Gateway siting is the design.** Because devices are heard by any gateway in range, gateway placement is a coverage exercise, not a pairing exercise: antennas high and outside metal cladding where possible, one gateway's cell overlapping the next so a single failure thins coverage instead of deleting it, and backhaul (Ethernet where it exists, cellular where it does not) planned with the same care as the radio side. A site survey with a portable gateway and a test device at the worst positions — inside the tank farm's steel, behind the boiler house — is worth more than any propagation model at this scale.

**ADR is the capacity manager.** With adaptive data rate enabled, the network server pushes each static device toward the fastest data rate and lowest transmit power its link supports, which is simultaneously the battery policy and the capacity policy: a network whose devices sit at SF7 has several times the airtime headroom of the same network at SF12. The specification's own guidance is to leave ADR on for stationary devices (with its back-off behaviour when conditions degrade) and to disable it for mobile ones, whose changing attenuation the algorithm cannot track. In practice this means the commissioning task is verifying, a week after installation, that devices have actually settled at the data rates their positions should allow — a device stuck at SF12 on a good path is a placement or antenna fault announcing itself.

**Airtime is the budget everything draws on.** Do the arithmetic once and the design rules stop being mysterious. At DR0 in Europe the channel runs at 250 bit/s, so a single maximum-size frame — roughly 60 bytes on air — occupies it for on the order of two seconds. Under a 1% duty-cycle limit, that one message is a measurable fraction of a device's hourly allowance, and the same seconds are subtracted from the gateway's downlink budget when an acknowledgement is sent. Multiply by a shift's worth of reporting and the conclusions write themselves: report at the slowest rate the process tolerates, keep payloads to the values that matter, confirm only the messages whose loss has a consequence, and let ADR keep devices off the slow data rates wherever the link allows. None of this is a defect in the technology — it is the explicit trade LoRaWAN offers: spectrum nobody licenses, in exchange for discipline nobody can automate away.

## Where LoRaWAN fits — and where it does not

LoRaWAN fits **distributed slow telemetry**: tank and silo levels across a tank farm, temperatures in warehousing, utility and effluent metering, pump-run and door states, environmental monitoring around a site perimeter — readings that change slowly, tolerate minutes of latency, and were previously read by a person walking a route. It fits **retrofit sensing** where pulling cable to each point is the project's whole cost. It fits multi-building campuses and utility sites where one operator can run gateways as its own infrastructure.

It does not fit **control** of any kind — no update guarantee, no bounded latency, downlink rationed by duty cycle. It does not fit **dense high-rate sensing**: vibration waveforms, power-quality data, anything whose raw form is larger than a few hundred bytes a minute. And it does not fit **as a plant's only wireless story**: inside process units, [WirelessHART](/protocols/wirelesshart/) and ISA100.11a meshes carry the instrument layer with managed schedules and per-device diagnostics, while LoRaWAN covers the scattered remainder.
